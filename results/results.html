Assignment 4, Part 1: Results¶

Concept bottleneck models for diagnosing skin lesions, using the derm7pt dataset.

Setup: dermoscopy images, ResNet-18 for g, one linear layer for f, 30 epochs, 3 random seeds, and λ = 1 for the joint model. Results are on the 395 test lesions, averaged over the seeds. The code is in project4.ipynb.

Terms used

  • Balanced accuracy: the average hit rate across the 5 diagnoses. Guessing gives 0.20. This is the main score, because the diagnoses are very uneven.
  • Melanoma sensitivity: the share of melanomas the model finds.
  • Melanoma specificity: the share of non-melanomas it correctly does not call melanoma.
  • Intervention: replacing predicted concepts with the true ones, as an expert would.

Main findings¶

  1. The bottleneck cost performance. Balanced accuracy was 0.55 for the standard model with no concepts, 0.51 for joint, 0.47 for sequential and 0.37 for independent.
  2. Concept quality does not explain the differences. Independent and sequential share the same concepts (0.63) yet differ by 0.10 in diagnosis score. Joint has slightly weaker concepts (0.62) but the best diagnosis score.
  3. Joint seems to get its score by using the concepts to carry diagnosis information. It scores 0.97 on training data from its own predicted concepts, but only 0.41 from the true ones.
  4. Interventions helped independent the most and made joint worse. With all 7 criteria corrected, independent rose from 0.37 to 0.60, sequential from 0.47 to 0.55, and joint fell from 0.51 to 0.40.
  5. So the best model at first is not the most correctable or explainable one. Joint predicts best on its own, while independent responds best when a doctor corrects its concepts.
  6. None of these models is fit for clinical use. They miss about 40% to 50% of melanomas.

Each explanation below is our best reading of the evidence. We did not test the causes separately.

2. Choosing λ for the joint model¶

We chose λ on validation data using the first seed: the best diagnosis score among models with a concept score within 0.05 of the best. λ = 0.1 scores slightly higher on diagnosis, but its concepts are far worse (0.44), so it would not give usable explanations.

What we see

  • Order: standard (0.55), joint (0.51), sequential (0.47), independent (0.37).
  • Clear differences: standard beats sequential and independent, and joint and sequential both beat independent. The 95% ranges for these exclude 0.
  • Not clear: standard against joint, and joint against sequential. Their ranges include 0, so these could be chance.
  • Plain accuracy tells a different story. Joint and standard both reach 0.68, against 0.55 for always guessing the most common diagnosis. Sequential (0.54) falls just below that guess, because its weighted training gives up common cases to find rare ones.
  • Melanoma: sensitivity is only 0.48 to 0.59, so all the models miss many melanomas. Joint is the most cautious, with the lowest sensitivity (0.48) and highest specificity (0.94).
  • Independent struggles with rare diagnoses: it often calls BCC and SK melanoma, and MISC a nevus.

Somewhat. Correct diagnoses come with about 0.6 more correct concepts out of 7 in all three models, so the link is real but weak.

Hard concepts help independent (0.37 to 0.42), because its f was trained on clean 0-and-1 inputs and is thrown off by uncertain predictions. They hurt sequential (0.47 to 0.41), whose f learned from uncertain predictions. Joint barely changes, so its extra information is not in the fine detail of the probabilities.

This is the most important table for understanding joint:

  • Its concepts and f depend on each other. On training data, joint scores 0.97 from its predicted concepts but 0.41 from the true ones. Independent does better with true concepts, as it should.
  • Its predictions hold more diagnosis information without being more correct. A new f scores 0.54 on joint's predicted concepts and 0.47 on g's, even though joint's concepts are no more accurate.

Together with 4.2, this suggests joint signals the diagnosis through which value it picks. A concept such as "streaks: irregular" may then partly mean "the model suspects this diagnosis". That is a risk for explanations, because a doctor would read it as a statement about the image.

Joint also overfits heavily: 0.97 on training data against 0.51 on test data.

The melanoma weights broadly fit medical knowledge, with correlations of 0.84, 0.70 and 0.72 to the checklist points. Independent matches best, which fits with it learning directly from true concepts.

The checklist itself is a strong baseline. On true concepts it finds 94% of melanomas with 82% specificity, far better than any model here. On predicted concepts it falls to about 66% to 74% sensitivity and 72% to 75% specificity. So the weak point is reading the concepts from the image.

One caution: we don't know whether the experts knew the diagnosis when they labelled the concepts. If they did, the true concepts may look more useful than they really are.

What we see

  • Independent gains the most, rising from 0.37 to 0.60. It passes the standard model after 6 corrections, and also gains the most on melanoma sensitivity (0.59 to 0.75). This matches the paper's bird task, where intervention was most effective for independent models.
  • Sequential gains less, from 0.47 to 0.55, and only draws level with the standard model when all 7 are corrected.
  • Joint gets worse, from 0.51 to 0.40. Best-first order holds it level for about 5 corrections, and then it drops sharply. The paper reports that interventions help, so this differs from it. Our explanation is the one from 4.2: correct concepts remove the diagnosis signal that its f relies on.
  • Order matters for few corrections. After 2 corrections, independent gains 0.08 in random order and 0.10 in best-first order. Sequential gains under 0.02 in either, and joint gains 0.01 at best.

Check against draft use case 2: "correcting at most 2 criteria raises balanced accuracy by at least 5 points" is met by independent only.

We ruled out a coding error: a separate calculation by hand gave the same result for joint.

6. Limits to mention¶

  • Settings were not tuned on the real data. The joint model's result could change with other settings. We tried only three values of λ, on one seed. We also set the learning speed for its f after a test on fake data, because the first value was too slow for f to learn.
  • Sequential is trained on over-optimistic concepts. g scores 0.92 on training data but 0.63 on test data, so its f learns from concepts that are better than it will see later.
  • Small numbers: there are few test lesions of some diagnoses, and only 3 seeds. The 95% ranges cover the choice of test lesions only.
  • Image type: we used dermoscopy images, where the 7-point criteria are defined. Ordinary clinical photos may give different results.
  • No patient ID: one patient could have lesions in more than one split.
  • Corrections here are perfect. Real doctors would make mistakes and disagree, so real gains would likely be smaller.
  • Decision rule: the models pick the most likely diagnosis. A lower bar for melanoma would find more melanomas but raise more false alarms. We did not test this.
